\section*{Question 1}

\subsection*{1a.}
Wien's Displacement Law relates the wavelength at which a blackbody is most radiant to the inverse of its temperature. It is defined under the assumption that the object is a perfectly radiating blackbody, which is a typical assumption for analysing most stars. It follows that

\begin{equation}
    \lambda_\textup{max}=\frac{2.898\cdot10^{-3}\textup{m}\cdot\textup{K}}T.
\end{equation}

On the t=1.43 days graph in Figure 1, the wavelength that is emitted at the highest flux (intensity) is approximately $1.2\cdot10^{3}$ angstroms, or $1.2\cdot10^{-7}$ metres. Hence, the temperature of the object 1.43 days after the explosion is $\approx2.415\cdot10^{4}$ K.

\subsection*{1b.}
Wien's Displacement Law essentially tells us that an increase in the wavelength (lengthening) which corresponds to the brightest flux leads to a decrease in temperature. We can see that over time, the maximum wavelength is getting longer while the maximum intensity decreases, which means the temperature is decreasing over time. Using the same approximation as in Q1a, the temperature between the first and last epoch has decreased by approximately $1.84\cdot10^{4}$ K, where the temperature after 24.01 days is $\approx5796$ K.

\subsection*{1c.}
We start to observe a significant deviation from the blackbody fit around the 14.94-day epoch. Beyond this there is a very significant deviation. This implies that the blackbody fit is no longer a reliable tool to estimate the temperature of the body.

In a more physical sense, the breaking down of the blackbody asumption implies that the supernova is losing opacity as it expands and cools. We start to lose the continuum emission of a perfect blackbody and see the individual emission lines start to dominate as the supernova becomes optically thin.

\subsection*{1d.}
The flux at $10^4$ angstroms of the object over time actually remains fairly constant. We can see that the last few data points, and even the blackbody fit, for the most part remain close to the $10^{-17}$ flux unit mark. The only noticeable shift is a slighy increase at first followed by a slight decrease later.

For a blackbody, flux is directly proportional to temperature and the solid angle. Assuming the flux remains mostly constant, and the temperature is decreasing, the solid angle must be increasing over time. This physically represents the expansion of the supernova. Finding an exact size would require knowledge of the distance to the supernova.

\subsection*{1e.}
Based on the previous estimates and given values we have a temperature of 24150 K, an $f_\lambda$ of $10^{-9}$ erg s$^{-1}$ cm$^{-3}$, a distance of $9.2\cdot10^{26}$ cm, and a wavelength of $10^{-4}$ cm (all in CGS units). The solid angle is calculated by

\begin{equation}
    \Omega=\frac{\pi R^2}{d^2}.
    \label{eqn:omega}
\end{equation}

We can then use this value where

\begin{equation}
    f_\lambda=B_\lambda \Omega.
    \label{eqn:flam}
\end{equation}

To get $B_\lambda$ using our flux $f_\lambda$, we want to convert the provided $B_\nu$ formula to be per unit wavelength. This is given as

\begin{equation}
    B_\lambda=B_\nu\frac{c}{\lambda^2}=\frac{2ckT}{\lambda^4}.
    \label{eqn:blam}
\end{equation}

Alright, working backwards we can start with finding $B_\lambda$ using Equation \ref{eqn:blam}. $c$ and $k$ are provided constants, and we have the temperature and wavelength. Again, all values are in CGS. We have

\begin{equation*}
    B_\lambda=1.998\cdot10^{15}\textup{ erg s}^{-1}\textup{ cm}^{-3}\textup{ sr}^{-1}.
\end{equation*}

Putting $B_\lambda$ and the approximate $f_\lambda$ into Equation \ref{eqn:flam} will get us the solid angle

\begin{equation*}
    \Omega=5.004\cdot10^{-25}\textup{ sr}.
\end{equation*}

And lastly Equation \ref{eqn:omega} will give us the radius of the supernova, which comes out to

\begin{equation*}
    R=3.67\cdot10^{14}\textup{ cm}\approx5276\textup{ solar radii}.
\end{equation*}

\section*{Question 2}

\subsection*{2a.}
The approximate value of $f_\lambda$ at 3 microns is $0.8\cdot10^{-21}$ erg s$^{-1}$ cm$^{-2}$ \r{A}$^{-1}$. This is equivalent to $0.8\cdot10^{-20}$ W m$^{-2}$ $\mu$m$^{-1}$. The energy of a single photon at $\lambda=3$ microns is given by

\begin{equation*}
    E_\textup{photon}=\frac{hc}{\lambda}=6.62\cdot10^{-20}\textup{ J.}
\end{equation*}

We can divide the flux by the energy of a single photon to obtain

\begin{equation*}
    \textup{Flux Density} = \frac{f_\lambda}{E_\textup{photon}}\approx0.12\textup{ photons s}^{-1}\textup{ m}^{-2}\textup{ }\mu\textup{m}^{-1}.
\end{equation*}

\subsection*{2b.}
It is given that the primary mirror diameter of JWST is 6.5 metres. Hence, the circular collecting area $A=\pi(\frac d 2)^2$ is approximately 33.18 m$^2$. The counts in units of photons per pixel would be the number of source photons per second per square metre per micron (Q2a) multiplied by the collecting area, the exposure time, and the linear disperson (microns per pixel). This works out as

\begin{equation*}
    \textup{Counts}=0.12\cdot33.18\cdot33612\cdot0.014\approx1874\textup{ photons/pixel}.
\end{equation*}

\subsection*{2c.}
The ideal signal to noise, if dominated by Poisson noise, is given to be

\begin{equation*}
    \textup{SNR}_\textup{max}=\frac{N}{\sqrt{N}}=\sqrt 1874\approx43.
\end{equation*}

Looking at the figure, the observed noise spans roughly $0.4\cdot10^{-21}$ around a value of $0.8\cdot10^{-21}$, resulting in a signal to noise ratio of 2.

\subsection*{2d.}
The observed signal to noise ratio was far lower than the idealised case. In reality, there is much more background radiation coming from the sky as well as thermal energy from the telescope, which all produce more noise. Another key reason is the read noise, which is introduced when counting the charge per pixel. Another physical limitation is the small slit size on the NIRSPEC which means that lots of the galaxy light won't be detected. All these reasons contribute to a much smaller signal to noise ratio.
